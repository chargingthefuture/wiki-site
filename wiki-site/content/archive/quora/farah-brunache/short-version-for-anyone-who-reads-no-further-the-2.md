Short version, for anyone who reads no further:

The network is at 89 people. A working economy needs people who can do about 650 different things. Those 89 cover 156 of them. That is about a quarter of the list. No trade has closed yet, and there are open requests nobody has answered.

The rest of this explains what that means.

What we have right now

The network is at 89 people. 41 signed themselves up. The other 48 have a community-generated profile — the app labels it that way and names who made it, which right now is me. Anyone can have theirs removed, and nobody has.

A working economy needs somebody who can do each of about 650 different things. Plumbing. Bookkeeping. Wiring a house. Fixing a car. That is the full list.

Those 89 people cover 156 of those things.

156 out of 650 is 24 percent. About a quarter.

No trade has closed yet. Not one job. Not one ride. Not one room. The index that counts finished work sits at zero.

The board is not empty though. Open requests are sitting on it right now — people asking for things that nobody has answered. Added up they come to 69 in what the app calls value waiting to happen: what those posts would come to if every one of them closed. It is a measure of interest, not achievement, and it is not money, not a price, and not redeemable for anything.

More people are asking than offering. That is not a failure and it should not be a surprise. Taking away a person’s ability to earn is the first thing done to anybody in this situation, so a group of survivors starts out heavy on need and light on capacity. The gap closes as people who can do things show up.

Two numbers, and they do not match

Two numbers describe where we are. They point in very different directions, and that is the interesting part.

The first number is people. The goal is 5 million members. We have 89. On that count we have barely started.

The second number is skills. The goal is all 650 covered. We have 156. On that count we are a quarter of the way there.

Same 89 people. Two completely different answers.

By headcount, almost nothing. By what the network can actually do, a quarter.

Why the second number matters more

Say you need a plumber.

If nobody in the network is a plumber, it does not matter how many members there are. You still have no plumber.

Now say one plumber joins. Now the network can do plumbing.

That is what coverage means. It is the list of things the network can do at all.

A network with 2,000 members who all do the same three jobs is not an economy. It is a mailing list. But 89 members covering 156 different trades is a real structure, even at this size. It can do 156 things, and each one is now something a member can get from another member.

Coverage is also the slow part. You cannot advertise your way to a welder. Either a welder joins or the network cannot weld. Every one of those 156 needed a real person with a real trade to show up and say so.

Adding people is the easier half. And every new member now arrives into a network that already does 156 things. That is a very different offer than the one the first ten members were given.

Where the $300 billion comes from

The big number comes from three smaller ones. All three are public. You can look up every one.

Number one: 5 million members. That is about the size of Finland.

Number two: 40 out of every 100 members work. That rate is Finland’s too, from their own public data. Forty percent of 5 million is 2 million workers. The other 3 million are still members. They are family, children, or people who joined for a place to live or a way to get around. They just do not trade a skill.

Number three: $150,000 per worker, per year. That one is Singapore. Take Singapore’s total economic output. Divide it by the number of people actually working there. You land near $150,000.

That third number is not a salary. Nobody takes home $150,000. It is the value of what one person’s work adds in a year.

Now multiply. 2 million workers × $150,000 = $300 billion.

That is the whole model. Finland tells you how many people there are and how many of them work. Singapore tells you what one worker in a skills economy produces.

One note on the five million. It starts from roughly two million in reported figures and adjusts upward for under-reporting. Then I rounded up.

The reason is specific rather than convenient. Normal under-reporting adjustments assume the ordinary reasons people stay quiet. Here, not being believed is a designed part of the method. It works by arranging things so that a complaint sounds like instability. So the usual adjustment is almost certainly too small.

The scope is rough in both directions. It comes from reports over roughly the last fifty years, so it counts people who have since died — some of natural causes, some by suicide, some killed. It also leaves out everyone before that window, and this goes back a great deal further than fifty years. One of those corrections pulls the number down. The other pushes it up. Neither one gets settled by arguing about it.

Which is the whole point. It is a guess, not a finding, and its job is to be replaced. The app is the instrument. As people join, list, and trade, a real number comes out of the data and this guess gets thrown out. I laid out where it came from when I first built the model. I am not going to re-argue it in every post.

Where we are today: 89 workers × $150,000 = about $13.4 million.

And again: none of that has happened. No trade has closed. That figure describes what the listed skills would come to if they were used. It is a measuring stick, not money.

The number that actually comes next

5 million is the far goal. It is not the next step. Treating it as the next step makes this look much longer than it is.

Here is the next step.

89 people cover 156 skills. That is about 1.75 skills each.

494 skills are still empty.

At that same rate, about 280 more people would fill the entire list.

280 people. Not 5 million.

That is a floor, not a promise. Some new members will list skills that are already covered, so the real number is higher. Nobody can say how much higher yet. But the size of it is the point. Filling the list is a job for hundreds of people, not millions.

How many people should end up in each trade has not been decided. It is too early for that. Coverage comes first.

And you do not need any of this to be finished. What one person needs is work, a place to live, and a way to get around, from people in this. That is a local number. It depends on how many members are near you, in your trades. It is far smaller than any national number, and some areas will reach it long before others.

The national count tells you how the whole thing is going. It tells you almost nothing about your street.

Why Singapore

Singapore has about six million people on roughly 730 square kilometres. No oil, no farmland to speak of, not enough fresh water — it imports water and most of its food.

At independence in 1965 its income per person was a few hundred dollars a year. Today it is among the highest in the world.

It was built on the only asset available, which was the skills of the people who lived there. That was the stated strategy of the people running it: no resources, so develop the population. Everything else followed from treating human capability as the thing of value.

The model is calibrated against Singapore rather than against a country with land and minerals for that reason. It is not a claim that we will replicate it. It is that a population of about the size we are aiming at, holding nothing but its own skills, reached that scale in roughly two generations starting from less than we have now.

And Singapore is not unusual

Estonia came out of Soviet occupation in 1991 with 1.3 million people — fewer than the 5 million on this page — no capital, and infrastructure that did not work. It built a functioning modern state largely in software, because software was what it could afford. Digital identity for every citizen, government services online, national online voting years before far richer countries attempted it. Being poor shaped the design. There was no money for buildings, so they did not build buildings.

Ireland spent the 1980s with unemployment near 17 percent and emigration as the ordinary plan for young people. Inside one generation it went from one of Western Europe’s poorest countries to one of its wealthiest. Its headline output figures are inflated by multinational accounting, which is why Ireland publishes a second, more honest measure — and the transformation is still large on that one.

Botswana at independence in 1966 was among the poorest countries on earth, with a population close to the two million on this page. It went on to one of the longest runs of sustained growth recorded anywhere.

Botswana is also a check on the model rather than only an example. Its population is a whole population — infants, children, the elderly, everyone not looking for work. Roughly a third are employed and around 40 percent are in the labor force at all. It produced that record with well under half its people participating.

The model on this page assumes exactly the same thing. It does not assume everybody works. It takes 40 percent participation from Finland and applies it to five million members, which is where the two million working figure comes from. The most obvious objection to a projection like this — that it counts people who will never trade anything — was priced in before anyone raised it.

The qualifier that does still apply is the one on every other part of this page: listed is not traded. A member who lists a skill and never uses it is not working either. Until trades start closing, all of this describes a structure rather than an economy.

To say it plainly, because these examples invite a misreading: this is not a country and nobody here is building one. No territory, no citizenship, no government, no intention of any. What is borrowed from those cases is arithmetic, not political form.

The reason the reference points are countries is that countries are where the data is. Nobody has assembled a large distributed member economy yet, so there is no dataset for one. There is a dataset for what a few million people holding nothing but their own skills can produce, and what it says is that the number is large and the timeline is about two generations, often less.

If none of that reached you

For a lot of readers it will not have, and there is a reason worth naming.

Someone who has been ground down for years does not experience their situation as one state among several possible ones. They experience it as how things are. That is not faulty reasoning and it is not weakness. It is the predictable result of a long enough run of things not working, and it is the exact condition Specterati harassment is built to produce. Isolation, no money, no traction, repeated over years, stops feeling like a description of a period and starts feeling like a conclusion about reality.

The tell is that it is also the one belief the whole arrangement needs you to keep. Everything described in these posts stops working on a person who treats their situation as temporary and acts accordingly.

I am not going to argue anyone out of that, and the arithmetic above will not either. But the arithmetic does not require your morale. Adding a skill to a list is not an act of hope. It works exactly as well when it is done by somebody who expects nothing to come of it.

There are 89 people on that map and 156 squares filled. Very little of that got done by anyone who was feeling hopeful at the time.

Tolerable

The description of what this is for was written sixty years ago by someone summarizing what organized labor had produced: not mere survival, but a tolerable life.

That is the goal. Not a figure with a B after it. Work that does not disappear when somebody makes a phone call. Housing that does not depend on the goodwill of a stranger who can be reached. A ride to where you need to be. Enough margin that a bad month is a bad month instead of the end of everything.

Eighty-nine people. A hundred and fifty-six trades covered. Nothing closed yet, requests waiting, and the arithmetic above is all anyone has to go on.

It is a real position, it is honestly stated, and a quarter of the map is filled.
