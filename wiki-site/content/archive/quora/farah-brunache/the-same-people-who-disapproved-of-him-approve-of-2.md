The Same People Who Disapproved of Him Approve of Him Now

There is a way to tell whether a population actually changed its mind about something, or whether it kept its position and the label moved. It is not a matter of interpretation. It is arithmetic, and it works on any case where you have polling from two points in time plus polling on the underlying question.

The cleanest documented example in American life runs from 1966 to now. I am using it because the polling is public and unusually complete, and because the same measurement can be pointed at something closer to home by the end of this. One thing it establishes along the way, in the subject's own words: the people who preferred quiet over justice were not sorted by race. They were sorted by what they had to lose.

The numbers

In 1966, Gallup asked Americans what they thought of Martin Luther King Jr. Sixty-three percent viewed him unfavorably. Two years later he was dead, and a Harris poll taken shortly before his death found a majority believed he was hurting the cause he led.

Today he polls above ninety percent favorable. There is a federal holiday. There are more than nine hundred streets.

That is a swing of roughly sixty points. Here is the part that matters: it was mostly the same people.

Population opinion moves in only two ways. Either the people holding the old view die and get replaced by people who never held it, or the same people change what they say. From 1966 to the mid-1980s, somewhere between a quarter and a third of the adult population turns over. Hold the survivors at their old views and make every single new adult favorable — the most generous assumption available, and false — and you still cannot get past about fifty-three percent. You cannot reach ninety without a third of the entire adult population personally reversing.

There is a second way to check it. If this were generational, the old view would still be sitting in the old cohort, and polling today would show the people who were adults in 1966 still disapproving while the young pulled the average up. That pattern is not there. Approval is near-universal across age groups. The people who said "unfavorable" in 1966 are inside the ninety percent now.

So they changed. The question is what changed.

They were not persuaded

If people had actually been argued into agreeing with him, their positions on what he asked for would have moved along with their opinion of him. Same population, same period, so it is testable.

It did not move. Opposition to reparations has held at roughly two-thirds. A guaranteed income — which he called for by name in his last book — has never approached majority support. Opposition to the specific methods he used, boycotts that cost local businesses money and occupations that shut things down, is as strong now as it was then, every time a live one runs.

Approval of the man rose about sixty points. Approval of what the man asked for moved approximately zero.

Persuasion does not produce that pattern. Only one thing does: the name stayed and the contents were swapped.

What was taken out

The version most of us were taught is a man who gave one speech about a dream, opposed segregation, believed in nonviolence, and won.

What is missing from that version is everything he actually did.

Montgomery was not a speech. It was 381 days of about forty thousand people refusing to pay bus fares, cutting the bus company's revenue by roughly two-thirds, with a carpool network of some three hundred cars run out of dispatch stations so people could still get to work. The city went to court to break the carpool, because the carpool was the machinery that made the boycott survivable.

Birmingham in 1963 was timed deliberately to the Easter shopping season and paired with a buying boycott, designed to strangle downtown merchants until the city broke. It was the business community that came to the table, not the politicians.

Operation Breadbasket ran standing boycotts against companies that would not hire Black workers. In Chicago he moved his family into a slum apartment and organized rent strikes against landlords.

In his last book he called for a guaranteed annual income for every American. He said the country needed "a radical redistribution of economic and political power." A year to the day before he was killed, he called the United States government "the greatest purveyor of violence in the world today," and roughly 168 newspapers denounced him within days — the Times calling it self-defeating, the Postsaying he had diminished his usefulness "to his cause, to his country, and to his people."

He died in Memphis backing a sanitation strike. The night before, he told the audience to pull their money out of downtown banks and boycott Coca-Cola, Sealtest milk, and Wonder Bread by name. He was in the middle of organizing a plan to occupy Washington until Congress passed an economic bill of rights.

Sort what survived from what was cut and the rule is exact. What survived is everything that costs nothing to agree with. What was cut is everything that moved money.

Who prefers order

There is a type of person who agrees with the goal and objects to every available method of reaching it. They accept that the harm is real. What they have is a concern about the timing, or the tactics, or the tone — a concern that is always specific, always sounds reasonable, and is always replaced by a fresh one the moment the first is answered. No method ever arrives that they endorse.

What produces this is exposure. Whether a person prefers quiet depends on what the interruption costs them measured against what the harm costs them, and that arithmetic runs identically for everyone. Someone with a position to protect protects it. There is nothing else in the mechanism, and any account that sorts these people by who they are rather than by what they stand to lose will predict wrong.

King described this type from the Birmingham jail in 1963, and made a claim about it that is still the most useful thing in the letter: the greatest obstacle was not the Klansman. It was the person "more devoted to 'order' than to justice" — the one who "paternalistically believes he can set the timetable for another man's freedom." The open opponent was never the problem. The problem was the person who agreed and would not move.

Order, in that phrase, means the undisturbed surface of ordinary life. Stores open, streets clear, nothing in the paper. It is defined entirely by the absence of visible friction and says nothing about whether anyone is being harmed. Harm that is quiet counts as order. Objecting to it counts as disorder.

This is also why the composition of the people involved never matches what anyone expects. Preferring order is not a function of having a lot. It is a function of depending on the arrangement, and dependence runs the entire length of the ladder. Someone with a position protects the position. Someone with almost nothing protects the little they have, or takes what is offered, because with no margin the cost of refusing is total. Those look like opposite situations and they are the same relationship to the same arrangement.

So the sorting principle is not a job title, not a rank, not an income, and not a race. It is who depends on order. That one predicts well. Everything else predicts badly, which is why people keep looking for the participants in the wrong places and concluding there must be an agency behind it — because no agency they can name would have recruited the people who are actually doing it.

Why any of this matters to you

If you are dealing with organized harassment — many people, coordinated, over years — two beliefs are probably costing you more than anything else. The case above takes both apart, and you do not need a second example to do it.

The first is that your experience is unique. Look at what has already been established here. The record of a specific person was rewritten at national scale while he was alive. Most of the country came to believe a version of him that was false — that he had damaged his own cause, that he was working for a foreign power — and the rewrite outlived him by sixty years and is still the version taught in schools.

None of that was hidden. It ran in newspapers. A hundred and sixty-eight of them denounced a single speech within days of it being given, and the largest papers in the country printed that he had ruined his own usefulness. The rewrite was done in the open, by ordinary institutions doing ordinary work, and it took.

None of it requires you to believe anything about your own situation. It is a documented case in which what a person was understood to be got replaced wholesale, against someone with more visibility, more allies, and more press access than you or I will ever have. Whatever you are dealing with, the pattern of it is not without precedent, and the fact that people around you accept a version of events you know to be false is not evidence that you are wrong about what happened.

The category has also been acknowledged formally. The UN Special Rapporteur on Torture's 2020 report to the Human Rights Council (A/HRC/43/49) addressed coordinated remote harassment as a category, noted the volume of allegations the mandate had received, and argued that they warranted investigation rather than dismissal. That report does not validate any individual claim and carries no count of how many people are affected. What it does is establish that the description exists in a document nobody has to take on faith.

The second belief is that this is "the government." I would let that one go, and not because the state is innocent.

Look again at the sixty-three percent. Most of the country disapproved of him on its own. Newspapers were calling him a communist and running editorials saying he had damaged his own cause. Eight clergymen published a letter commending the Birmingham police for their calm — the same department that turned dogs and fire hoses on children weeks later. His own organizational allies were telling him he was hurting the movement. Not one of those people was on a payroll, and none of them needed instructions. Whatever else was happening, it had a population willing to carry it, and the carrying is what did the damage.

"The government" implies an org chart — an agency to name, a policy to repeal, an office whose closing ends it. What is actually there is thousands of ordinary people making small decisions, some of them holding official positions, most of them not. That is a harder adversary, not a softer one. There is nothing to subpoena.

But it is also the reason there is something you can do.

The third door

Most people who work this out land in one of two places, and both are dead ends.

The first is deciding that people are simply bad, and going quiet. I understand the appeal and I think it is wrong, and the proof is the machinery itself. If gaslighting a person were ordinary human behavior, none of the apparatus would be needed — no recruitment, no money, no scripts, no handlers, no pretexts, no starting small and escalating. Nobody builds a machine to make people do what they would do anyway. Every part of that structure exists to overcome resistance, and how elaborate it is measures how much resistance there is. Even Milgram, remembered as proof that people are naturally cruel, shows people sweating, laughing uncontrollably, and begging to stop while complying under engineered conditions. That is not what ordinary behavior looks like. It is what coerced behavior looks like in people whose instincts were still working underneath.

None of which is an excuse. Explaining why someone participated is not the same as moving the responsibility off them. They chose repeatedly, and one of those choices was to accept a story that let them not look. "I only did a small part" was rejected as a defense at Nuremberg, and it should be rejected here.

The second dead end is waiting for justice — for the investigation, the lawsuit, the exposure that makes it stop. I am not telling anyone to drop that. I am saying that whatever you are owed, you have to eat and stay housed and get to appointments while pursuing it, and that nothing about the structure above suggests an institution is going to move first.

There is a third option, and it comes directly out of the mechanism. If participation runs on money and dependency rather than on people being bad, then what people will do to you is a function of what they need from someone else. Change what people depend on and you change what they are willing to do. That is not a feeling about human nature. It is the same reasoning the boycotts ran on, and the boycotts worked.

Which is why sentiment was tolerated and withdrawal was not. Speaking against poverty was free then and it is free now. Millions do it, including the institutions that cause it, and nothing happens to them, so it cannot be what marks anyone. What drew the response was operational: people withdrawing their money and their labor, in numbers, for a long time.

What we are building

An economy among members. Work, housing, rides, hiring, trade. The first three are the three problems named above. Invite-only, and it costs nothing to join. It is not a support group and it is not a campaign. It is the practical business of reducing how much of your life runs through people who have shown you what they will do.

It is built distributed on purpose. No single building, no headquarters, no one location that can be burned, and no one person whose removal ends it. A member in Kansas trading with a member in Florida has no address for anyone to visit. Everything above argues that the pressure is distributed, and the answer to a distributed thing is not a center.

You do not have to agree with anything above to use it. You do not have to accept any account of who is doing what to you or why. You have skills, you need housing, rides, and work, and there are people here who need what you have. That is worth joining whether or not anyone is targeting anyone.

If you can live inside the existing economy, live in it. Most people do, and there is no argument here that they should not. If you cannot, or if you have looked at what it takes to stay in good standing there and decided the cost is too high, there is a second one being built, and it is open to you.

Nobody buys insurance because they expect a fire. If your situation stays mild, you have gained a network of people who trade with each other. If it does not, you are already in one.

Further reading

For anyone who wants the documented precedents.
- US Senate Select Committee to Study Governmental Operations (the Church Committee), final report, 1975–76. Documents COINTELPRO as official, funded policy: informants recruited from inside target communities, forged correspondence, planted press stories, and campaigns to break up marriages and cost people their jobs.
- Zersetzung, and East German Directive 1/76. The formalized method of working a target's surroundings rather than the target — turning friends and spouses, arranging professional failures, producing a run of small unprovable events, with the stated aim of fragmenting and isolating the person so that complaining would itself read as instability. Operational files survive in the German federal archive.
