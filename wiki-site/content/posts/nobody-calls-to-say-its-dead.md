Yesterday I had a conversation with somebody on the app who I had already concluded was a Specterati operator. I had been keeping our contact short for a while. They kept asking for phone calls that turned out to be about nothing, and after enough of those I stopped treating the requests as ordinary.

They asked for another call. On it, they told me the app is dead. Nobody is on it. They are getting no value from it.

Then, in the same conversation, they asked me to ask them to stay.

Then they asked for advanced permissions, so they could help bring more people in. They have not brought one.

## Three tells in one call

Take the first on its own. A person who opens an app, finds nothing they want, and gets nothing out of it does not schedule a call to say so. They stop opening it. Nobody in the history of software has picked up the phone to inform a developer that their project has no users. That sentence is not a review. It is something you say to a person you want to stop.

The second one is stranger than the first and gives it away. If the place is empty and worthless, there is nothing to be asked to stay for. Wanting to be invited to remain somewhere you have just called dead is not a position anybody holds. It is a move, and the move needs the first sentence to set it up.

The third says what the first two were for. Admin access, to go and get users — from somebody whose contribution to date is zero invitations. The sequence is the app is failing, I am still here, let me in deeper so I can fix it. Each part exists to make the next one sound reasonable.

I want to be exact about what I am claiming. I am not saying anybody who criticizes the app is an operator, and I am not saying this because the criticism stung. Criticism is useful and I get plenty of it. What I am describing is a shape: an unprompted declaration that the project is finished, delivered by voice, to the one person whose continuing is the thing at stake, followed immediately by two asks that only make sense if the declaration is believed.

## There are no advanced permissions to give

The request was not going to be granted, and not because I had made up my mind about the person. There is nothing there to grant.

The app has one thing a member can do with another member: provide material value. Somebody needs a ride, a repair, a place to stay, an hour of a trade they do not have. Somebody else has it. They arrange it between themselves, both sides come out of it with something real, and then it is finished. The exchange settles and there is nothing left over.

That structure is deliberate, and it is the part that frustrates a person who came to do something else.

There is no staff to get to. On a platform of any size, an operator's most effective route is not their own account — it is somebody on the inside who can restrict an account, remove a post, or lose a report. Several of my own accounts elsewhere have gone that way. Here the moderation surface is small on purpose, and it is one person.

There are no ads to buy and no feed to place anything in. There is nothing to pay for at all.

You cannot follow a person around. There is no follower graph to build, no way to attach yourself to somebody's activity and be present wherever they are.

Swapping between identities does not work either. Nobody is required to use a legal name, but moving between accounts in a way meant to deceive gets caught, and account approval is a person looking at a real account with a public history behind it.

So what is left for somebody whose purpose is disruption is to provide material value like everybody else, which is the one thing they will not do. That is why the conversations kept wanting to be phone calls. The call is the only place left where the structure does not apply.

## About the app being empty

The version of this that is worth answering is the one where a real Targeted Individual passes through, sees little happening, and leaves thinking the same thing.

So let me put the numbers where the app's own screens put them, and then say what they mean.

People sign up and are approved one at a time. The skills map is built differently — I research people individually, from skills they have stated publicly in their own words, and add them to the Directory. Being listed and having an account are separate things, and most of the list was built for people rather than by them. Three survivors have helped me directly this year. None of that passed through the app, and all three are on the Directory. The listing is what made them findable; the help happened between people.

Now the part about pace.

Traffickers run large communities and they run them on grift. The population is achieved by theft, by coercion, and by violence — people are moved into place, people are kept in place, and people who want to leave find out what leaving costs. When an operation like that needs a crowd to appear somewhere, a crowd appears, because the people in it were not asked.

I am building something else: a way for people to live outside the psyop and keep living. A thing built without stealing anybody, without extorting anybody, and without coercing anybody does not fill on command, because there is no command. There is nobody I can order to show up on a Tuesday to make a screenshot look better. There never will be. Everyone here shows up when they want to and when they can, and that is the arrangement working rather than the arrangement failing.

There is also the ordinary part, which I will not dress up. This is a product with a lot of features, in its third version, serving a group that is marginalized specifically by organized crime. For an ordinary app at this stage, the activity would be unremarkable. For this one, add that a serious amount of effort goes into keeping the people it serves isolated from each other. Both things are true and neither is a surprise.

Two years ago, telling survivors that a community of their own could exist got two answers. That it was a pipe dream, or that it was dangerous — that gathering would make everybody's targeting worse. Some of the people who said that have since changed their minds. That change took two years and it happened without anybody being stolen.

## What I did about the account

I stopped the calls. Not by announcing it, and not by accusing anybody of anything, just by declining. What is left is writing, in the Commons group chat, where it is in public and on the record and I can answer at a time I choose. When something is out of line I say so plainly, and the behavior adjusts for a while.

The account stays. I do not delete accounts on suspicion, and I want to be clear that this is a position rather than a technicality. Being removed from a place on somebody's suspicion, with no process and no appeal, is done to survivors constantly — it is a large part of why I am building this at all. I am not going to import it here on day one because I am confident about a read. I am confident about this read. Confidence is not the standard.

The standard is conduct against the rules, which are short and which apply the same way to everybody. If this person crosses them, the account goes for what they did, and that will be legible to anybody who looks.

There is one more reason to leave it be, and it is practical. Organized crime does not give up when a route closes; it finds another one. Watching what somebody tries next, in a place where trying it does not work, is how the next mitigation gets built before it is needed. We have always had to adapt faster than the people working against us. That has not changed and it is not going to.
