It is an invasion of privacy, and you are right to call it that.

## Knowing who started it

An ex can start it. Who put you on the list explains how you got here, and it matters up to a point. Past that point, everybody who joins runs the same schemes whoever started it, which is why knowing the name does not end it by itself. [It does not matter who put you on the list](https://chargingthefuture.github.io/chargingthefuture/article/wiki-site/who-put-you-on-the-list) goes into that.

## Proving it

One incident can be denied. A pattern is much harder to deny.

Write down what happens, with the date, as it happens: what, when, where, and who if you know. [ClickLog](https://app.chargingthefuture.com/apps/click-log) takes about fifteen seconds an incident, and your notes are not shown to anyone else here. Names belong in your own record, not on a public page. Over months, the record is what shows the pattern, to you first.

If you are thinking of reporting it, [Who to report it to](https://chargingthefuture.github.io/chargingthefuture/article/wiki-site/who-to-report-it-to) is my answer to that question. None of this is legal advice.

## Taking your accounts back

Accounts come back one at a time, from a device you trust.

- Start with your email. Every other account resets its password through it, so whoever holds your email can take the rest back after you.
- Give each account its own new password, and turn on two-step sign-in, where a code is needed as well as the password.
- Every major account has a list of the devices signed into it. Sign out every one you do not recognize.
- Check the recovery phone number and the backup email on each account. Somebody who changed those can take the account back after you change the password.
