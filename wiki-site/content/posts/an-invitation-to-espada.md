The app keeps a list of thirteen jobs that a community has to be able to fill before it can run at all.

Water and sanitation. Building and infrastructure. Food. Health. Safety. Making and repair. Education. Operations. Six more. It is not a wish list — it is the set of things where, if nobody can do it, the lights are on and the place still does not work.

No one person fills thirteen jobs and nobody is asked to. What a list is for is seeing which of them are thin.

## Espada

Espada (https://www.quora.com/profile/ESPADA-18), in Detroit, Michigan. The Directory profile is at https://app.chargingthefuture.com/apps/directory/profile/42506e2d-d81a-4455-b3fd-bd76984ecdf0 and it carries three specializations — plumber, CCTV, small appliance repair.

Their own description of the work is longer than the tags: small engine repair, installing CCTV, appliance repair, handyman, plumber's apprentice.

Apprentice is the word they used, and it is the one worth keeping. The tag on the listing reads plumber, which is the label the catalog had to hand. Somebody learning a trade is not the same as somebody who has finished learning it, and saying otherwise would be putting a claim in their mouth.

Those five kinds of work land on five different jobs out of the thirteen.

Plumbing is its own job on that list and also part of another. The app describes the Water & Sanitation team as drinking water and wastewater once the taps exist — sources and wells, treatment, testing, distribution, repairs — and separates it from Build & Infrastructure, which installs the thing in the first place. One of those puts the pipe in. The other is why the water coming out of it is safe a year later.

Small engine repair and appliance repair are Making & Repair, which the app describes as keeping equipment running past the point where a supplier would replace it. That sentence was written before anybody read Espada's listing, and it is a description of what they already do.

Handyman work is Operations & Maintenance — the maintenance rosters, the shared things that have to keep working, the jobs nobody has a title for.

CCTV sits in Safety & Security, which is the gate, the perimeter, and knowing what actually happened rather than arguing about it afterward.

## What the list actually holds

The skills catalog carries 657 entries — the set of things a working economy of about five million people needs somebody to be able to do. On September 14 the Directory's 147 people held 184 of them.

473 have nobody at all.

Some of the gaps are wide in a way that is easy to miss because the number is small:

- Water and sanitation: 3 skills held, out of 19 in the catalog.
- Energy and utilities: 3 held, out of 20.
- Public safety and justice: 1 held, out of 15.
- Manufacturing and industry, which is where making and mending sits: 11 held, out of 28.

Those are not competitive fields where somebody would be joining a queue. They are close to empty.

The only part of the catalog the Directory has finished is retail and services — all fourteen of its skills held. Everything else has room, and the rooms this listing opens onto are among the emptiest.

Which is where the word apprentice stops being a qualifier. Water and sanitation is three skills out of nineteen, held across a list of 147 people. A trade that thin is not short of masters — it is short of anybody at all, and the person currently learning it is the one who ends up holding it. On a finished list somebody early in a trade is behind. On this one they are the front of it.

## Why this is an invitation rather than an announcement

Espada did not sign up. Most people on the Directory did not.

The list was built by reading what people said about themselves in public and writing it down under their name, one at a time, which is why an entry is marked community-generated until its owner claims it — the listing says so on its face, and names me as the person who put it there. Saying that plainly is the honest part, and it answers the question anybody would be asking by now.

Claiming the listing is what makes it theirs rather than mine. It takes a free account and a few minutes, and after that they set what it says and can take it down.

Nothing else is required. They do not have to use the rest of the app, do not have to post anything, and do not have to agree with a word I have written about why any of this is happening.

If the answer is no, that is a complete answer and nothing about the listing changes. It can come down at any time, with no account involved anywhere in that. Nobody who has been asked has wanted theirs removed, and the offer has been real since the day the list went up.

## This does not run one way

An invitation that only asks is not worth much to somebody already carrying a full week.

Somebody who fixes what other people rely on needs things too, and usually needs them from a stranger. A ride that does not go through somebody who should not be driving. A room. An hour of a trade they do not have. Those are on the list as well, under other people's names, and the same listing that makes them findable is what makes those findable to them.

That exchange is deliberately the first thing here rather than the last. Asking somebody for something real is also what filters out the people who will never hand anybody anything.
