A question on Quora, saved on August 31, 2025:

> Why do some people quit being involved in gang stalking, and what consequences do they face for leaving?

The [Dictionary](https://chargingthefuture.github.io/chargingthefuture/article/wiki-site/Dictionary) carries the words I use for this instead.

## Why some leave

I do not know anybody's reasons from the inside, and I am not going to make them up. What I can describe is what the arrangement asks of people, and that is enough to make wanting out easy to understand.

Nobody is told at the start what it is for. The asks arrive later: steal this, sleep with that person, help ruin somebody. By then a reward has already been taken, or the person is in far enough that leaving costs more than staying. [The best description of it I have read](https://chargingthefuture.github.io/chargingthefuture/article/wiki-site/best-description-i-have-read) lays out that order.

Some of the people doing this enjoy it. The rest are in because of what the structure already gave them, and a person in that position can see what they are being asked to do without wanting to do it. Leaving is not one thing, though. How a person goes decides what it costs.

## The quiet way out

Most who stop do it quietly. They cut the target out of their life and say nothing. They expose nobody, and in return they keep what they were given, or they are simply left alone. Nothing is taken back, because nothing about the arrangement has been threatened.

That is leaving the target, not the network. Silence is the last thing it asks of them, and they give it.

## What the other way costs

Leaving any other way, by saying what they took part in or who asked them to, is where the cost comes in. It is things. The job, the apartment, the promotion, the standing that came with going along, and whatever was taken before the person understood what it was for. The favors come first, and once somebody has them the terms are plain. There is a debt held over them as well, and there is always another target to be assigned.

Losing a home is traumatic to the person it happens to, and I am not going to say otherwise. But none of it is their life.

## Never their life

Across these networks there is an understanding that a target can be robbed, injured or killed and nobody will answer for it, and that the people who did it will be rewarded instead. No such cover extends to the people taking part. Ordinary crime still applies to somebody who is not a target, so what a participant risks by leaving is property, a job, a reputation, and never their life.

That difference is the arrangement, not luck, and it is why the cost of leaving is a reason and not a justification. Somebody who stays to keep what they were given is weighing their things against another person's life. The cost being real does not settle that.

## The person who never went in

There is an opposite case, and it should not be confused with this one. Somebody who will not take part in the first place is not a participant, and the protection above has never covered them.

Two people I knew would not take part, and proved hard to turn into targets. They were killed.

## What would change it

[No one to traffic](https://chargingthefuture.github.io/chargingthefuture/article/wiki-site/no-one-to-traffic) takes this further. Right now the exit does not exist: every route out of the network runs back through it. An economy where survivors are not destitute and not alone takes away what makes a person worth trafficking, and then the people who never wanted to do this stop having to.

That is not forgiveness. Somebody who did it under pressure still did it.
