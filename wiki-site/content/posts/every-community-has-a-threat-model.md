Earlier today I published [the case for a gated community and who could plan one](https://chargingthefuture.github.io/chargingthefuture/article/wiki-site/a-gated-community-and-who-could-plan-it). This post is about the part of that conversation that deserves its own page: threats, and what serious people do about them.

## Objections are not the problem

I do not think objections to a survivor community are a problem. Communities are a serious undertaking and the objections deserve serious answers.

The problem is how the objections arrive: the threat is presented, and the other side never is. History has shown that oppressed in-person communities can work despite disruption — that is the record, and I went through it in the last post. Violence against communities will always happen. Both of those things are true at once, and a person who only ever hears the first half is not being informed. They are being frightened.

Presenting the danger with no plan beside it is not caution. It is fear dressed as caution, and its effect — whoever intends it — is that nothing ever gets built.

## Every community has faced this

Every community since the beginning of time has had to consider threats. Man-made and natural. Fire, flood, drought, crime, war, sickness, the neighbor who wants the land. There has never been a community that did not face them, and there has never been a community that was built by waiting for the threats to end first.

A survivor-only community faces the same question every community has ever faced. Not a new one.

And the threats we face are not special to us. The places run by the people doing this face fire, crime, betrayal, and infiltration like everyone else does. The difference between a community that lasts and one that does not has never been the absence of threats. It has been the quality of the plan.

## The plan is the answer

What matters is planning and preparedness, built on the community's own threat model — a plain list of what could go wrong, how likely each thing is, and what the community does about each one. Every functioning town, congregation, and campus on earth runs on one, written down or not.

You put your best foot forward in the plan. And when something goes wrong — something will — you fix what broke and update the next plan. That is not failure. That is the entire method, the same one every fire code and every emergency drill came from. A plan is not a promise that nothing happens. It is the reason something happening does not end the community.

Refusing to build a community because something could go wrong is like refusing to step outside for fear a tree falls on you. The fear names a real thing. Trees do fall. And staying indoors forever is still not a reasonable response to trees.

## The comparison nobody makes

I have never lived in a survivor community. My life is threatened every day anyway.

That is the comparison the fear skips over. The question is not "community with threats, or safety without them?" Safety without them is not on the table — not for me, and not for the people asking on Quora whether such a community exists. The question is whether living among people who understand what is happening, with a plan, is more dangerous than living scattered among the people doing it, with none. Stated plainly, the claim that it gets worse when you leave the people harming you is not logical.

## The tool

This is why the planning tool exists. The Community tab sorts the people already on the skills map into the ten teams a community's planning document needs — and one of the ten is Safety & Security, whose whole job is the threat model: the gate and perimeter model, emergency plans, drills, and an incident process that respects member privacy. The full walkthrough is in [the last post](https://chargingthefuture.github.io/chargingthefuture/article/wiki-site/a-gated-community-and-who-could-plan-it).

Plan first, in public, with the people who can do the work named next to the work. That is the other side that never gets presented.
