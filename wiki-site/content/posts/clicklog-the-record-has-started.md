Six days ago I wrote that a shared ClickLog entry feeds something that has never existed anywhere: real-time trend data on organized harassment, reported by the people it happens to rather than assembled by outsiders after the fact.

That was a description of a thing that had not happened yet.

## What the shared view reads now

The window is the last ninety days. Everything in it falls between August 13 and August 23, 2026.

- Shared incidents: 20
- Members reporting: 2
- Members who logged more than one: 1
- Days with activity: 5
- Countries: 2
- Areas, about 11 km each: 3

That is the screen, stated the way the screen states it.

## The count that matters is two

Not twenty. Two.

One person logging their own incidents has a diary. It is a useful diary — it is the whole of what ClickLog does privately, and it is worth keeping whether or not anyone else ever sees a line of it. But a diary cannot tell you whether what is happening to you is happening to anyone else, and that is the question underneath almost every conversation survivors have.

Two people in two countries is the smallest version of an answer. It is the difference between a record and a comparison.

The trends show a pattern one person cannot see. That is the entire reason the shared view exists.

## What twenty entries do not establish

Everything in that view is self-reported and nothing in it is corroborated. Twenty entries from two people show what those two people say happened over eleven days. Read as anything more than that, it becomes a stick to beat every member who logged something.

Two gaps stay open, and both are on the roadmap rather than in the product. The report cannot say what a member did about an incident or what came of it, so nothing in it shows whether anything helped. And no entry has a second source attached.

Saying that plainly is what makes the rest of it worth reading. A number that admits what it cannot carry is more useful than one that does not.

## Nothing in that view came out of anyone's notes

Worth repeating whenever a figure gets published, because the figure is the moment people wonder what else went with it.

The note on an incident is never shared. It is left out of the query rather than filtered afterward. The exact location is never shared — only the area of about 11 km, which is why the screen counts areas instead of naming streets. Who logged what is never shared: the view can say two people are behind those entries, never which two.

Sharing is off unless you turn it on, and there is a per-incident choice on top of the setting. Delete an incident and it leaves every future report.

Log privately forever and never share a line, and ClickLog still does its first job completely.

## The standing argument

The UN Special Rapporteur on Torture's 2020 report to the Human Rights Council, A/HRC/43/49, addressed coordinated harassment as a category and said the volume of allegations warrants investigation rather than dismissal.

A body that says investigate needs data. Trend data is the form a submission takes. No individual account has to be adjudicated for an aggregate to be worth looking at, which is exactly what makes an aggregate worth building.

## The data is not mine to keep

This is the part I want to be plain about.

The shared totals are published. They are not a private holding that this project sits on and rations out. Anyone who wants to use them — a survivor checking whether their area appears, a researcher, a journalist, someone drafting a submission — can take them and use them as they like.

That posture is deliberate and it is the opposite of how numbers about people in our situation have always worked. Every count of us so far was made by someone standing outside, describing us to a third party, on terms we never saw. This one is made by the people it happens to, and it is theirs.
