## Peace-Battle — two rates from 1903

W. E. B. Du Bois, "The Talented Tenth", in *The Negro Problem*, 1903. The full book is public domain at https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/15041 and is committed alongside the game so the figures can be checked.

He counted two thousand college-trained people at the top of a chain. Those two thousand reached fifty thousand teachers. Those fifty thousand reached nine million. Twenty-five times over, then a hundred and eighty times over. Those two rates are the only numbers the game runs on.

You decide each round how much goes to teaching and how much to the work itself. Put everything into the work and you reach three hundred and sixty thousand people. Teach first and you reach nine million — same effort, same rounds, twenty-five times the reach. That second split is the one Du Bois recorded, and the game says so when you land on it.

They are his figures, written in 1903 to make an argument rather than to pass an audit. He was not describing a hypothetical; he was describing what a small number of trained people had already done.

There is a preset for where this project actually stands. As of August 28 it reads 101 people recruited, covering 160 of 657 skills. The rates do not change — only the number at the top of the chain does, and it is about a twentieth of what he started from.

Nobody argues with the arithmetic once they have done it themselves.

## Peace-Battle 2 — a map, and nobody wins alone

The second one is a knock-off of Pandemic, with the polarity turned around.

In Pandemic a disease spreads across a map of cities and you race to contain it. Here what spreads is isolation — a place where somebody needs something and nobody findable can do it. Four people with skills move against it. People are never the contagion. Being cut off is.

Sixteen real cities, sized by population. Four actions a round: move along a link, or help where you stand. Helping where the need matches your skill does twice as much, and clearing a place puts it in the Directory — covered from then on, its people counted, isolation never returning to it. Then it grows in two more places, and one already at its limit cuts off and pushes into every place beside it. Six cut off and it is over.

Cover the whole map and thirty million people can find somebody. It is cooperative. There is no winner, and nobody gets there by beating anyone.

That is the difference between the two games. The first shows what a decision about where to put effort is worth. The second makes you produce the outcome where everybody is reachable, and it needs the same thing the real version needs: somebody has to be findable before they can be asked.

## Why a board and not an essay

Lizzie Magie patented The Landlord's Game in 1904 to teach Henry George's argument about land. She could have written an essay; she built a board. Parker Brothers bought the patent in 1935 for five hundred dollars and no royalties, Charles Darrow patented Monopoly the month after, and it sold to the world with Darrow named as its inventor. She died in 1948, unknown.

That is the method these borrow, and it is the reason for building a game rather than writing another post. An argument you have to play is harder to wave off than one you read, because you produce the outcome yourself.
