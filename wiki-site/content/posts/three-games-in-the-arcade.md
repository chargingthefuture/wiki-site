## Why a game

Lizzie Magie patented The Landlord's Game in 1904. She was a stenographer at the Dead Letter Office who had read Henry George, and she wanted people to understand what happens when land concentrates in a few hands. She could have written an essay. She built a board instead, and said so plainly in her 1924 patent application: the object was to illustrate "how under the present or prevailing system of land tenure, the landlord has an advantage over other enterprises."

In November 1935 she sold the patent rights to Parker Brothers for five hundred dollars and no royalties. The month after, Charles Darrow patented Monopoly, and Parker Brothers sold it to the world with Darrow as its sole inventor. She died in 1948, unknown. Asked how she felt about it, she said that if the idea reached the people shaking the dice, the whole business would not have been in vain.

That is the method these games borrow. An argument you have to play is harder to wave off than one you read, because you produce the outcome yourself.

## Peace-Battle — where the numbers go

The first one replays something that actually happened. In 1903 a group set out to train teachers rather than to do the work directly. Two thousand people trained fifty thousand, who reached nine million.

You allocate each round between teaching and working, and watch what the split is worth. Put everything into the work and you reach three hundred and sixty thousand people. Put it into teaching first and you reach nine million — the same effort, the same rounds, twenty-five times the reach. The preset that matches how most effort is allocated today lands at four hundred and fifty-four thousand.

Nobody argues with the arithmetic once they have done it themselves.

## Peace-Battle 2 — a map, and nobody wins alone

The second one is a knock-off of Pandemic, with the polarity turned around.

In Pandemic a disease spreads across a map of cities and you race to contain it. Here what spreads is isolation — a place where somebody needs something and nobody findable can do it. Four people with skills move against it. People are never the contagion. Being cut off is.

Sixteen real cities, sized by population. Four actions a round: move along a link, or help where you stand. Helping where the need matches your skill does twice as much, and clearing a place puts it in the Directory — covered from then on, its people counted, isolation never returning to it. Then it grows in two more places, and one already at its limit cuts off and pushes into every place beside it. Six cut off and it is over.

Cover the whole map and thirty million people can find somebody. It is cooperative. There is no winner, and nobody gets there by beating anyone.

That is the difference between the two games, and it is why the second one exists. The first shows you what a decision about where to put effort is worth. The second makes you produce the outcome where everybody is reachable, and it turns out to need the same thing the real version needs: the right person has to be reachable, and somebody has to be findable before they can be asked.
