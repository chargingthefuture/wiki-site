The phrase gets used loosely, mine included. It has a definition, and the definition is narrower than the way it is usually used. Here it is, and what follows from it.

## The definition

The United Nations Convention against Transnational Organized Crime, adopted in Palermo in 2000, is the definition most countries signed. Article 2 reads:

> "Organized criminal group" shall mean a structured group of three or more persons, existing for a period of time and acting in concert with the aim of committing one or more serious crimes or offences established in accordance with this Convention, in order to obtain, directly or indirectly, a financial or other material benefit.

A serious crime, in the same article, is one punishable by at least four years in prison. Structured does not mean a chain of command. Commentators on the convention point out that it needs neither formal roles nor a hierarchy. The text is reproduced at https://jus.uio.no/english/services/library/treaties/04/4-05/un-transnational-organized.html.

The FBI's glossary, in a version archived in 2004, said the same thing in American terms, and added how such a group keeps its place:

> any group having some manner of a formalized structure and whose primary objective is to obtain money through illegal activities. Such groups maintain their position through the use of actual or threatened violence, corrupt public officials, graft, or extortion.

That page is at https://webharvest.gov/peth04/20041015231332/http:/www.fbi.gov/hq/cid/orgcrime/glossary.htm.

## What it does not say

Neither definition says a government is in on it.

Corrupt public officials are in the FBI's list, as one of the group's methods, next to violence and extortion. That is the group reaching into an office and buying one person in it. It is something done to a government, not something a government does with the group. If an entire state were running the operation, the word for it would not be organized crime. It would be state crime, and people would call it that.

John Mixon, a writer who is not a Targeted Individual and was describing American cities in general, described the other half. In some communities, he wrote, organized criminals, the descendants of gangs, have real influence over social and economic life, and they know their limits and operate within them to avoid crackdowns. I quoted him in [The economics that oppress us can save us](https://chargingthefuture.github.io/chargingthefuture/article/wiki-site/economics-oppress-save).

That is not protection. Enforcement is a limited resource spread across priorities, and a group that stays under the line that draws attention is left alone because it costs more to pursue than to ignore. A group that has to stay under a line is a group that something can still stop.

## Who wants it gone

So far, the people I have seen argue for tearing governments down outright, or for saying it is only the government and nobody else, have been people taking part in this.

The definition explains why. Enforcement is the thing that limits organized crime, so the arrangement it would choose is no enforcement at all, except its own rules. A case that it is only the government points every survivor at the one institution that can still crack down, and away from the people standing next to them.

None of that makes any government clean, and none of it asks you to trust one. It asks you to use the words for what they mean. The [Dictionary](https://chargingthefuture.github.io/chargingthefuture/article/wiki-site/Dictionary) carries the names I use for the people doing this, and [Unite around what?](https://chargingthefuture.github.io/chargingthefuture/article/wiki-site/unite-around-what) is what I think to do about them that does not need the question of who runs it settled first.
