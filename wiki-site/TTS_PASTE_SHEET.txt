LevelUp is SkillUp now. Same thing, clearer name.

Seats are filling. Enrolling holds a deposit of 50 credits, and you get every one of them back — they are returned to you as your trainer signs off each milestone, and if you leave the cohort the entire remaining hold comes back at once. So the course costs nothing in the end. What it asks for is 50 credits you can put down while you are in it.

I did not pick those three. SkillUp generates its own cohort recommendations from the distance between what the skills map holds and what people are looking for, and those came back as the widest gaps. People are enrolling against them now, and nobody is scheduled to teach any of them.

Screenshot: The Trainers tab in SkillUp, headed Trainers — survivor-advocates who lead the cohorts. The list is empty. So this is a Request For Skills, trainer edition.

What running one involves.

You set the schedule and the cadence. Not me, and not the app.

Remote by default, because the cohorts are open globally — somebody who enrolls is as likely to be five time zones away as five miles. Run it inside the app or on whatever video tool you already use. The app does not care which one, and I am not going to recommend one.

In person is acceptable on one condition: it stays open to anybody who enrolled and cannot be there in person. A call running alongside it, a recording, a second sitting — whatever it takes. A cohort does not become a local one because the trainer is local.

You do not need to be a professional instructor. You need to know the work well enough that somebody who does not can follow you.

Two things the app checks rather than anybody approving you. Your own Directory listing has to carry a skill belonging to the occupation the cohort trains, and the listing has to be one you have claimed. Or nominate a trainer you know.

You do not have to be the one teaching. If you know somebody who could run one of these, nominate them in SkillsHunt — a name, where they are, what they can do. If the nomination is accepted, you receive credits for it.

ti-radio

TI Radio: a week of live discussions, and anyone can read it.

Survivors host live discussions. Until now the only way to say one was happening was to announce it.

An announcement can point at one time. It cannot hold a schedule. And it cannot let somebody else put themselves on one.

So now there is a page that does both.

What it is.

A week of live discussions, laid out in front of you.

The day is cut into 90-minute slots. Ninety minutes divides a day exactly sixteen times, so every day has the same sixteen starts. Nothing drifts. The grid runs seven days ahead.

Each row shows the time, who is hosting, and what it is about. The slot happening right now is marked "On air."

Every time is printed in your own timezone, and the page names the timezone it detected. You do not have to do the arithmetic.

An empty row is one somebody can take.

Reading it needs no account.

This is the part worth saying plainly, because it is not how the rest of the app works.

You do not sign in. You do not get verified. You do not join anything. You open the page and read the schedule.

Almost every other screen here is behind approval, and for good reasons I have written about before. This one is not, and the reason is who it is for. The people it was built for have not joined anything yet. Telling them to sign up before they can even see when a discussion is happening asks for the thing at the end and gives nothing at the start.

So the schedule is open. Anyone can link to it. Anyone can plan around it.

Taking a slot.

Hosting is different, and it does need an approved account.

An approved member presses an open row and writes what the discussion is about. They can add a few sentences for somebody deciding whether to come. The slot is then theirs, and their handle and subject appear on that row for everyone — including people who never sign in.

First come, first served. No queue, no application, nobody approving the topic.

At the time they booked, they and whoever turns up meet in Chyme.

Why hosting is gated when reading is not: putting your name on a public schedule is a commitment to turn up.

The limits, and why they are there.

You can hold three slots in any 24 hours. Not three per calendar day — any 24-hour stretch. Three late one evening and a fourth after midnight is refused, the same as four in one afternoon. Otherwise one person could take a week.

You can give a slot back any time before it starts. It goes straight back to the guide as open.

You cannot give it back once it has begun. By then people have turned up.

An admin can remove any slot and is asked why. That is me, and it is one person.

What it is not.

It is not Mutual Time. That is the other scheduling thing in the app, and it works the opposite way. An admin starts it. Everyone votes on when they are free. The app picks the hour with the most overlap. One meeting comes out of it, and only an admin can begin one.

TI Radio asks nothing. A member takes a time. A week of discussions comes out of it, side by side.

Both exist. They answer different questions.

It also does not own the room. The talking happens in Chyme, which keeps its own audio and its own records. TI Radio holds the schedule and nothing else. It never records who listened.

Why a schedule rather than an announcement.

An announcement reaches whoever is looking the day it goes out.

A schedule sits there. Somebody who finds it on a Tuesday can see what is on Thursday. Somebody in another timezone can find the one slot that works for them. Somebody who is not ready to join anything can still turn up and listen.

And it is not mine to fill. I am not booking sixteen slots a day and calling it a station. The rows are empty until somebody takes one, and what appears there is whatever survivors decided to talk about.

What's Your Hangup?

If something about the Skills Economy stops you, I want to know what it is.

Not as a challenge. I am asking because I cannot answer an objection nobody says out loud, and because some of them are going to be right.

You can write it under this post.

Here is the one I hear most, and the one I want to answer properly.

"It is not a real job".

Underneath that is usually something like: I had a career, I was good at it, and this looks like a step down.

I understand it. I want to put the record next to it.

Destitution comes after the proof, not before.

Look at when it happens.

A person gets educated. Gets hired. Gets good. Gets promoted. They are a productive participant in the global economy, by any measure that economy uses.

Then it starts. The references dry up. The role is restructured. The contract is not renewed. The next interview goes strangely. It compounds until there is nothing coming in.

The destitution arrives after the proof of capability, not instead of it. That order matters, and it is the same order for almost everybody this happens to.

If what they wanted was your work, they would have let you keep the job.

So your participation is not what they are after.

This is the part worth sitting with.

Whatever is being extracted here, it is not your labor and it is not your output. They removed those on purpose, from people who were demonstrably good at producing them.

Five million people leaving the global economy would not dent it. Not a little — not at all. And they already push survivors out of it, which tells you they do not want that participation either.

This is the opposite of what was asked for before.

Dr. King's last campaign was about money. Not symbolism, not seats — wages, jobs, and a union for sanitation workers in Memphis, and a Poor People's Campaign aimed at economic demands. The ask was to be let into the economy on fair terms.

He was killed in the middle of that work.

What happens to Targeted Individuals now is not that. There are deaths, and they are arranged to look like something else. But the public killing of somebody for making an economic demand is largely not the method anymore.

I can think of two reasons. One is that killing somebody in the open is no longer something a person can do and walk away from. The other is that what is wanted out of us changed — that the daily suffering of somebody living in destitution is now the thing being harvested, and a dead person stops producing it.

I do not know which. I am not sure it matters, because both land in the same place: they do not want you in that economy in any sense.

Their method runs on humiliation.

Every scheme, when you strip it down, is built to make a person feel small in front of others. Shamed. Discredited. Not believed.

Knowing that changes the shape of the question.

If the method is humiliation, and the removal from work is part of the method, then staying loyal to an economy that already removed you is not principle. It is the last part of the humiliation still doing its job.

So what exactly is the hangup?

What working among them actually looks like from here.

I want to be straight about the alternative rather than strawman it.

Until there is justice, there is no reliable path to working among them except the one they allow. And what gets allowed is narrow: roles where somebody else sets your hours, watches your day, and decides your pay.

That is not a judgment about the work or the people doing it. Every one of those jobs is real work and somebody has to do it. The point is which jobs stay available to a person who is being targeted, and why those particular ones do.

They are the ones with the most control in them.

The Skills Economy is built the other way round.

You set the level of engagement. One ride a month, or nothing for six months, or every day.

Nobody schedules you. Nobody approves your rate. Nobody can remove you from it, because there is no gate for anybody to stand at — there is no second tier and no way to buy influence over another account.

And if you are ambitious, the ceiling is not set by whoever is willing to hire you. What's your 1%? runs that arithmetic for your own trade: one percent of five million survivors is fifty thousand people, and it works backwards from there to what serving them would be worth in a year.

That figure is not a promise. It is what the arithmetic says is available to somebody who decides to go after it.

Workforce tells you what a population of five million would look like. It never told you what you could do inside it, and a lot of people read the entire screen and conclude none of it is about them. What a feed is.

A feed is a plain list of what has been published here — each post's title, its date, a few sentences saying what it is, and its address. It is written for software to read rather than for a person.

Quora has erased my accounts forty-seven times.

Several people have asked, and the honest answer is that email is the thing most people use and also the thing most easily taken from me.

A mailing list lives or dies on whether the large mail providers think you are wanted. Once crossed, every subscriber stops getting mail, including the ones who asked for it. The service sending it removes the account and the list goes with it — which is the same shape as everything above, with a different company's name on it.

That is not a reason to never do it. It is a reason to build the thing that cannot be taken first, and to design the fragile one so that losing it costs me a send rather than the people.

I have written that nobody who joins owes me anything, and that I owe nobody either. That still stands, and this post is what follows from building the thing anyway.

What the network runs on.

It needs people who are destitute and alone.

So an economy where survivors are not destitute and not alone is not a comfort. It is a supply problem for them.

The part that reaches past us.

I have written about the three places this road ends, and about what I expect if a fourth route exists, in Something to lose. I will not restate the argument; the short version is that people driven to those endings do not only harm themselves, and fewer of them is a benefit to everybody standing nearby, targeted or not.

I cannot prove that in advance, and I am not going to pretend otherwise. It is what I expect, and it is one of the reasons I am doing this rather than something easier.

Notice what it is not. It is not a favor to anybody, and nothing is being asked in return. A person who has never been targeted gets the benefit without joining, without agreeing with me, and without knowing this exists.

And the other side of it.

This is the part I have not written down before.

The people doing this are not all enjoying it. Some are. The rest are in because of what the structure already gave them — the job, the apartment, the promotion, the thing they took before they understood what it was for — and because walking away would cost them those things. That is the arrangement I described in the earlier post: the favors come first, and once you have them the terms are plain.

I want to be exact about what that is, because it is a reason and not a justification. Losing a home is traumatic to the person it happens to, and I am not going to tell anybody otherwise. That difference is not incidental. Across these networks there is an understanding that a target can be robbed, injured or killed and nobody will answer for it, and that the people who did it will be rewarded instead. No such cover extends to them. Ordinary crime still applies to a person who is not a target, which is why what they risk is losing things and never losing their life.

Nobody in that position can simply walk away, because the debt is held over them and there is always another target to be assigned.

Unless there is not.

If this works, there is no one to traffic. Not fewer, not harder — none, because the material a person needs to be worth trafficking is what the economy takes away. A trafficker with no target has nothing to trade on and nothing being asked of them. The people who never wanted to do it stop having to.

It is not forgiveness, and it is not a deal. I said in the earlier post that following orders is not better than enjoying it, because the order is the same debt being collected one level up, and I have not changed my mind. Somebody who trafficked a person under pressure still did it.

What it is: the exit existing at all. Right now it does not. Every route out of that network runs back through the network, which is the same trap from the other side, and the people held there have as much reason as anybody to want this to succeed.

That is the argument, and it does not need anybody to be grateful for it. I am not owed. Neither is anybody else here. The work is worth doing on what it produces, which is fewer people with nothing left to lose, on both sides of the thing.

There is no returning.

Once a name is on the list it is never taken off. No method I know of stops this, mine included, and I have never met a survivor for whom anything ended it. Measuring your life against a normal that is not coming back does real harm, and it is worth naming exactly what the harm is. A person who is working toward restoration reads every ordinary incident as a failure of the plan — proof that they are not recovering, that they did something wrong, that it is hopeless. It is not. It is Tuesday, and the conditions did not change. A plan that only succeeds if the harassment ends is a plan that will tell you that you failed, every week, forever.

What does come back.

Here is what I have watched come back, in my own life and other people's. It is a short list and every item on it is specific, which is what makes it usable.

Income. Not the career that was taken — that one usually does not come back the way it was, and people who wait for it lose years. What comes back is the ability to earn, which is not the same thing. You still know what you know. Foundation is where people list what they can do and reach each other directly, and ServiceCredits settles an exchange in an internal unit rather than money, so somebody with nothing in a bank account is not out of the exchange.

Housing. LightHouse is rooms offered by survivors to survivors, which is the answer to the scheme that walks a destitute person back to relatives who were reached first.

People who know you. This is the one that changes the most and the fastest. Being on the Directory is what makes somebody findable, and finding two or three people who understand without being told is not a small recovery, it is most of one. Commons reads without an account, so it costs nothing to start there.

The ability to ask. This one is a skill rather than a circumstance, and it comes back with practice. Ask small, bring something, and do not measure it against equal — that is what a working exchange looks like between two people who both need things.

Your own account of your life. ClickLog is fifteen seconds an incident and your notes stay yours. A person with a dated record is not relying on memory under stress, which is exactly where this puts it, and memory rewrites the earlier version to match the later one.

What does not come back, and should not be promised.

Proof. The methods are chosen to be deniable one act at a time, and no amount of recovery produces the evidence that was never going to be allowed.

The people who were turned. Some of them were approached before you knew anything was happening. Expecting those relationships back is a way of staying in the same week for years.

The time. That is a loss and it is not repaired by anything on this list. It also is not the most interesting fact about you.

So what is recovery here.

Not restoration. Rebuilding, under conditions that are still running.

The measure that works is whose hands your week runs through. Count them. How many of the things you need — the room, the ride, the work, the trade — come through somebody who is part of this, and how many do not? That number moves, it moves because of things you can do, and it does not require the harassment to stop first.

Show up with your percent.

Most of a Targeted Individual's day is not theirs. It goes to the attacks and to what they force: moving again, watching the people around you, sleeping badly or not at all, and doing ordinary things that have been made slow on purpose. What is left at the end of it might be five percent. Some days it is nothing.

Five percent is not enough to reach a goal alone. A job in another state, a vehicle you can live in, a move that holds: each one is a chain of small steps, research and calls and follow-ups, done in order over weeks. The attacks land on the chain. A step gets missed, the order breaks, and it starts over. Anybody living this knows the shape of it, and knows that telling somebody in it to try harder is telling them to have a day they do not have.

The arithmetic changes when the five percent is not all one person's. Twenty members with five percent of a day each is a working day. Nobody in it had more time than they had. That is what the goal board runs on.

What it is.

PeerProgramming now opens on a Goals tab, and the tab is a board.

You post one goal with a finish line. You break it into cards: small tasks somebody else could do from a phone in under half an hour. Find three yards hiring near Dallas and add their numbers. Find three campers under six thousand dollars within two hundred miles of Tulsa. Ask one dealer whether they finance with no credit check.

The cards sit in three columns: Up for grabs, Doing, Done. You pick any one card, from anybody's goal. You do it, you post what you found, and it moves to Done with your name on it. The goal's owner reads the result and marks it "It helped", keeps it, or sends it back for somebody else. That is the entire loop.

There is no conversation on the board. A goal, its cards, and their results, and nothing else can be written there. The weekly sessions and the call are still in PeerProgramming.

A mill that made other people's socks.

Marc Cabot founded Cabot Hosiery Mills in Northfield, Vermont, in 1978. For decades it made private-label socks for retail chains such as Gap: other companies' names on its work.

In the early 2000s those contracts moved overseas, and the business nearly collapsed.

One product, made to last.

Ric Cabot, Marc's son, did not chase cheaper labor abroad. In 2004 he launched Darn Tough, with one promise: socks good enough that nobody needs to buy replacements. Every pair carries a lifetime guarantee. Wear one out, send it back, get a new pair, no receipt needed. Fewer than 1% are returned in a year.

Twenty years later the company employs more than 300 people across three Vermont sites, sells over 5 million pairs a year, and takes in more than $50 million.

The figures are from a review on Get Up and Go Camping, published December 15, 2025.

The math.

Everybody wears socks. Five million pairs a year is one pair for every person in the population the Workforce screen models.

The average contributor in What's Your 1% brings in $142,500 a year. At $20 a pair, that is 7,125 pairs. In a population of five million, that is about one person in 700 buying one pair, once, in a year.

That is the low end, and it is a living. The high end is Darn Tough's own number. Somebody can start at the average and decide later, or decide now, to aim at the top.

Darn Tough's turn came when its work moved overseas. For us that question is already settled. Targeted Individuals have been pushed out of the global economy, so there is no cheaper country to move the work to. What gets made here is made by people here, for people here.

picked-off-early

Picked off early.

Targeted Individuals bring up COINTELPRO all the time. It was the FBI's program for watching, infiltrating and discrediting American groups, and many survivors say it is what they are living in now. What I almost never see next to it is the name of its best-documented target: Martin Luther King Jr.

The people doing this to us do not draw that line, because drawing it admits what they are.

I think survivors leave it alone for a different reason: King's case admits this is not only a game a government plays. The FBI went after him, and so did private people who wanted him stopped. In a Gallup poll in August 1966, 63 percent of Americans held an unfavorable view of him, and neither government nor business counted itself among his friends. The FBI's part has been told at length, by survivors and in the Senate's own record. The private part is the one left out, and it is mine. I am not targeted by a government. Private groups use the same method, and mine started when I was five years old.

What the record says.

The Senate's Church Committee put the FBI's campaign against him on the public record in 1976. The Bureau wiretapped his home and offices, bugged his hotel rooms, and in November 1964 mailed him an anonymous letter, with a recording, urging him to kill himself. What organized crime means covers that, and why I call what happens to me organized crime rather than a government program.

The same report quotes a 1968 FBI memo listing the goals of its program against Black organizations. One of them was to "prevent the rise of a 'messiah'" who could unite them. The memo named King as one who could.

That is a plan to stop a leader before there is one.

Being called a leader.

I have been accused of being a leader. It is a strange thing to say as an insult, and I read it as the same plan, adjusted. King had years to build allies before anyone moved against him. The version I see now moves sooner: find the people who could organize others and cannot be controlled, and deal with them early, so an opposition that is not a grift never forms.

What he did without social media.

He had the national press and television, and the movement planned its actions around them. What carried it were methods that need no platform, and most of them can be built again.

1. Networks people already trusted. The movement ran through Black churches, which already had meeting rooms, phone trees, money and leaders people believed. It joined audiences that already existed instead of building one. The closest thing here is the Directory, and one person recommending another.
2. Hand-to-hand distribution. The share messages on the Peace Battle 2 page work the same way: a different one for each person, posted under their own name.
3. One campaign with one clear demand. Montgomery was the buses. Birmingham was the downtown stores and their jobs. Selma was the vote. Each had a place, an ask and a finish line.
4. Mutual aid that made it last. The boycott held for 381 days because the movement ran its own carpool, a few hundred volunteer cars with pickup points and dispatchers. the-count-measures-the-ending

The count measures the ending.

How many mass shootings happen in the United States each year depends on who is counting.

Gun Violence Archive counts any shooting where four or more people are shot, not counting the shooter: 408 in 2025.
The AP, USA Today and Northeastern University count mass killings, four or more killed: fewer in 2025 than in any year in two decades, 59 percent below the record 41 of 2019.
The FBI counts active shooter incidents, somebody trying to kill people in a populated place: 24 in 2024.
The Crime Prevention Research Center counts four or more murdered in a public place, not during another crime such as a robbery or a gang fight: between one and eight a year since 1998, and four in 2025.

Every one of those is correct for what it counts. Two choices set the size of the number: whether the wounded count, and whether the most common kinds of shooting are left out. A narrow count is not owned by one side. The lean is in which count a writer picks: the broad one is quoted to argue there are many, a narrow one to argue there are few.

What none of them count.

Every count is of an event after it happens. None records pressure toward a shooting that never happened, or an attempt that was stopped. The criminologist who manages the mass killing count calls mass shootings rare events: about 1 percent of US gun deaths, 100 to 200 lives in a typical year against roughly 17,000 gun homicides. That is a share of deaths after the fact, and every other count stops at the same place.

The hypothesis.

Mass shootings are counted per person in the population, so the published figures call them rare. That measures the outcome, not the pressure. The pressure toward one can be widespread while the outcome stays rare, because most targets never act. A rare result does not mean a rare effort. When thousands of people take part, coordinated targeting is common, even though the people targeted are a small share of the population.

I wrote in Something to lose that for many years they tried to get me to buy a gun and use it on a crowd, and that I did not. No count of mass shootings has a row for that.

Skills Economy and net zero.

The second hypothesis follows from the first: Skills Economy could, in theory, bring mass shootings to net zero.

The pressure works by closing off a person's options. Income ends, work is interfered with until it stops, the person is moved back into a family house (Why they want you living with family), and nobody around them believes what is happening. Destitution, a mass shooting and suicide are the endings left once everything else is closed.

Skills Economy reopens the options. Work is traded on what you can do, with people who are not taking part: Workforce shows which skills the community has, and the Directory lists survivors by what they can do. The goal board is where you put the 1% of the day that is left into small tasks toward somebody else's goal and post your own. A person with income, work and people who know them has something to lose, and the pressure has less to work with.

What "in theory" covers: the shootings the pressure produces. I said in Something to lose that I do not believe that is every shooting, and I cannot prove it is any. Neither hypothesis has been tested. Testing them takes records kept the same way by many Targeted Individuals, so the number pushed toward the ending can be set against the number who reached it.
